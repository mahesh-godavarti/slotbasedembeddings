# Mod vs Fixed: What The Latest Results Actually Show

The latest results make the situation clearer: on standard IID Speech Commands classification, the `mod` variants do not currently justify themselves as better classifiers than `fixed`. The newer experiments do not just show a small optimization gap; they point to a structural pattern. Residual mod variants mostly match fixed rather than beating it, removing frequency bias hurts mod, and making the frequency prediction network either larger or smaller does not help. At the same time, fixed benefits cleanly from architectural improvements like tighter local windows and bidirectionality. Mod also degrades more than fixed when sequence length increases (`hop=40`) or when training becomes less direct (`MP2`).

The most important interpretation is that the useful part of `mod` may already be the constant part. In the standard `ModTied` setup, the bias term in the frequency projection appears to be carrying meaningful "base frequency" information. When that bias is removed, performance drops. That suggests the model is leaning on a learned fixed prior plus relatively weak data-dependent perturbations, rather than extracting large gains from true input-adaptive frequency control. The residual-mod experiments reinforce that point: when modulation is explicitly anchored to a fixed base, the result is essentially parity with fixed, not a new capability advantage on the benchmark.

Because of that, I would stop using vanilla top-1 Speech Commands accuracy as the main test of whether `mod` is worthwhile. The current evidence says this task mostly rewards a strong stable local basis, and fixed frequencies are already a very good fit for that. Speech Commands is short, clean, and fairly stationary once the mel front-end is in place. That means it is not an especially favorable benchmark for demonstrating the value of input-dependent frequencies. If modulation is going to matter, it will likely matter under distribution shift, local nonstationarity, or mixed acoustic regimes rather than in the clean IID setting.

So the right way to demonstrate the capabilities of `mod` versus `fixed` is to change the evaluation target from "best average accuracy on the standard test split" to "best robustness or adaptability when the local spectral-timescale assumptions are violated." The cleanest experiment would be to train both models on standard Speech Commands and then evaluate on a grid of synthetic time-stretch and pitch-shift conditions. If `mod` really has a content-adaptive advantage, it should lose less accuracy as utterances are sped up, slowed down, or pitch-shifted. This would be far more informative than another round of IID accuracy comparisons.

A second strong direction is mixed-regime evaluation. Construct a test setting that combines normal speech with acoustically shifted variants such as time-stretched, pitch-shifted, reverberant, or noisier versions. Then compare not just overall accuracy but per-regime performance. Even if `mod` does not improve the average, it may win decisively on the shifted subsets. That would support a more precise claim: fixed is better for the dominant stationary regime, while mod is better when the appropriate local dynamics vary across examples or within examples.

A third useful analysis is per-example disagreement analysis between matched fixed and mod checkpoints. Instead of only comparing one final scalar, identify which examples are correct only for mod and which are correct only for fixed. Then bucket those examples by measurable properties such as speaking rate, duration, energy contour, approximate pitch, or SNR. If modulation is doing anything meaningful, it should show up there before it moves the headline average. This could reveal that mod helps on atypical or harder utterances even when the benchmark average stays flat.

A fourth direction is controlled nonstationarity within the utterance. If input-dependent frequencies are supposed to matter, then they should matter most when the "right" local processing basis changes over time. That suggests synthetic tasks where speech is locally time-warped, partially pitch-shifted, or otherwise altered within the same one-second clip. Fixed should struggle more when one static frequency strategy must cover the whole clip, while mod should have a better chance of adapting frame by frame.

The broader conclusion is that the latest results do not show that `mod` is useless. They show that `mod` is not the right tool to optimize the current benchmark objective. Fixed appears to be a better bias for clean, short, mostly stationary keyword classification after a mel front-end. If `mod` has a real advantage, it is likely an adaptability advantage rather than a raw IID accuracy advantage. The next experiments should be designed to expose that distinction directly.

The single strongest next experiment would be: train `FixedTied` and `ModTied` on the standard training set, then evaluate both on a grid of time-stretch and pitch-shift perturbations, and plot accuracy drop relative to clean test accuracy. If mod is genuinely adaptive, that experiment should reveal it quickly. If it still does not help there, then the evidence against mod for this family of audio tasks becomes much stronger.

## Updated Narrative After The July 18-19 Results

The more recent perturbation and phase-mode experiments change the emphasis of the story. The main result is no longer that input-dependent modulation might matter under the right conditions. The main result is that the fixed mel+cumsum architecture already works very well, and that this fixed design is the strongest and cleanest evidence for the approach.

The core claim supported by the results is that mel+cumsum is a strong temporal aggregation architecture for keyword spotting. Once a mel front-end is in place, the important problem is not necessarily to learn a fully adaptive spectral basis. The more important problem is to aggregate local evidence over time in an efficient and structured way. The fixed mel+cumsum models do that well enough to be competitive with, and in several settings better than, the CNN baselines.

Several architectural findings support this fixed-centered story.

First, fixed mel+cumsum is genuinely competitive. The best fixed variants reach very strong test accuracy, and the tied fixed models do so at a parameter count that is competitive with standard mel CNN baselines. This means the approach is not just an interesting variant or a fragile ablation result; it is a viable architecture in its own right.

Second, the most important design lesson is local-window depth, not wide-window aggregation. The results consistently show that many layers of tight local cumsum windows outperform fewer layers with wider windows. This gives a clean interpretation of why the architecture works: cumsum is a simple aggregator, so it performs best when used locally and repeatedly, with nonlinear gating between layers. In that sense, the architecture behaves more like a deep stack of narrow receptive-field operators than a single wide temporal integrator.

Third, tying is a success rather than a compromise. The tied fixed models preserve almost all of the performance of the larger untied models while using far fewer parameters. This is an important part of the overall argument because it shows that the architecture is structured and efficient. The good performance is not coming only from giving every layer large independent frequency banks and projections.

Fourth, bidirectionality improves the fixed model further. The bidirectional fixed variants outperform the unidirectional version, and the shared-parameter bidirectional model improves accuracy without increasing parameter count. This is a strong supporting result because it reinforces the main story: once the local cumsum mechanism is sound, giving it access to both past and future context yields another measurable gain. That makes the architecture look less like a one-off trick and more like a principled temporal building block.

Against that backdrop, the modulation story is now best understood as a negative result. Across the later experiments — uniform stretch, split-stretch, nonlinear distortion, phase-mode changes, and broader stretch-training regimes — `mod` does not deliver a reliable advantage over `fixed`. The earlier mild slow-side stretch effect does not grow into a robust pattern under stronger tests. In the most targeted adaptive tests, especially split-stretch, fixed and mod degrade almost identically. That strongly suggests that the win in this project is coming from the cumsum architecture itself rather than from input-dependent frequency control.

This is not a weakness in the project. It actually sharpens the contribution. The cleanest narrative is now:

"A fixed-frequency mel+cumsum architecture is a strong, efficient temporal aggregation model for keyword spotting. Small local cumsum windows, depth, gating, tying, and bidirectionality matter. Input-dependent modulation does not provide additional benefit on this benchmark, which clarifies that the main source of performance is the cumsum aggregation structure itself."

That framing is stronger than a modulation-centered story because it is simpler, better supported by the full set of experiments, and easier to communicate. The `mod` experiments are still valuable, but mainly as boundary-setting negative results: they show what does not matter nearly as much as expected, and in doing so they make the fixed architecture result more convincing.

## Why These Shifted Regimes Matter

The reason to focus on shifted regimes is simple: they test the thing `mod` is supposed to buy, namely adaptation. On the standard Speech Commands split, the task is short, fairly clean, and relatively well matched between train and test. Once the mel front-end is in place, a stable fixed local basis is already a strong fit. That means the benchmark gives `mod` very little room to demonstrate an advantage.

If `mod` is genuinely better than `fixed`, it should show up when the appropriate local processing changes across examples or within an example. Time-stretch tests adaptation to speaking rate: a fixed local frequency schedule is tied to one timescale, while `mod` should in principle retune when speech is compressed or expanded. Pitch shift tests adaptation to spectral placement: if harmonics or formants move, `mod` should have a chance to track that better than a fixed basis. Mixed-regime evaluation tests whether a single model can handle multiple local dynamics without needing a separate model for each regime. Controlled nonstationarity within an utterance is the most direct case of all, because the best local basis may actually change over time.

So these settings are interesting for two reasons. First, they matter practically because they probe robustness under realistic mismatch. Second, and more importantly for this project, they matter scientifically because they isolate the capability claim behind `mod`. Without these tests, the comparison is mostly asking whether an adaptive model can imitate a fixed one on a task where fixed is already near-optimal.

Reverberation and resampling error are good examples, but they probe slightly different things. Reverberation is especially interesting because it spreads energy over time and changes the local temporal structure. That directly stresses a fixed local aggregation scheme, so it is a plausible place for `mod` to help by adapting to a smeared effective timescale. Resampling mismatch is also useful, especially when it creates controlled timing and spectral distortions. That tests whether `mod` handles basis mismatch better than `fixed`.

It is useful to separate two kinds of resampling experiments. Clean time-stretch or sample-rate mismatch is a strong scientific test of timescale adaptation. Ugly resampling artifacts are still valuable, but they test broader robustness and artifact tolerance rather than the core adaptivity hypothesis. For a clean demonstration of what `mod` is for, the most targeted perturbations are controlled time-stretch, controlled pitch shift, and controlled reverberation. If the goal is broader practical robustness, then it also makes sense to add codec distortion, noisy resampling artifacts, background noise, and microphone or room mismatch.
